## Understanding-level scientific and instructional ledger

### A2.2 — Cell structure

#### A2.2.1 — The cell as the unit of life 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 39, PDF 45; Hodder printed 49, PDF 61. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Every living organism consists of one or more cells. A cell is the smallest organizational unit capable of coordinating life processes; many organisms consist of one cell, whereas multicellular organisms have cells organized into tissues and organs. A tissue is an association of specialized cells, not an alternative to cellular organization. Molecules such as DNA or proteins are vital components, but an isolated molecule cannot maintain the integrated processes of life.
- **Biological example / application:** If a sample is a new organism, predict a membrane-bounded cellular unit and describe an observation that could test the prediction.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-organization
- **Terminology:** cell theory; unicellular; multicellular; tissue
- **Misconception to correct:** A tissue is the smallest living unit. → Tissues are composed of cells; their integrated function arises from the activities of those cells.
- **Further original explanation:** Cell theory supports a deductive prediction: when scientists discover an unfamiliar living organism, they expect it to be made of cells. A freshwater Amoeba already demonstrates that one cell can perform many functions; a human muscle contains many cells organized in tissue. Viruses have genetic material and evolve, but lack independent cellular metabolism and are not counted as cellular organisms. The prediction is scientifically testable and must be reconsidered if strong contrary evidence emerges.

#### A2.2.2 — Measure the microscopic world 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 39, PDF 45; Hodder printed 53, PDF 65. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Prepare a thin temporary mount on a clean slide, use a suitable stain only when needed, and lower the coverslip carefully to reduce trapped air. Begin focusing at low power; use coarse adjustment cautiously, then fine focus, before moving to higher magnification. An eyepiece graticule must be calibrated for each objective using a stage micrometer. Record units, specimen context and technique, and take an image with a documented scale rather than assuming pixels correspond to a fixed physical length.
- **Biological example / application:** A 45 mm image represents a 15 µm cell. 45 mm = 45,000 µm; magnification = 45,000/15 = ×3000. A 5 µm bar on that print would be 15 mm long.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-scale
- **Terminology:** temporary mount; eyepiece graticule; stage micrometer; magnification; scale bar; resolution
- **Misconception to correct:** A label of ×400 stays numerically correct when an image is resized. → Printed or screen magnification depends on display size; a correctly attached scale bar remains useful after proportional resizing.
- **Further original explanation:** Magnification is image size divided by actual size, using the same units. If a 60 mm printed cell image represents an actual 30 µm cell, first convert 60 mm into 60,000 µm, then calculate 60,000/30 = ×2000. For a scale bar, calibrate image distance against a known specimen length: if 20 µm covers 40 mm on a print, a 10 µm bar must be 20 mm long on that same print. A digital display resize changes on-screen magnification, but a correctly embedded scale bar rescales with the image. Magnification is not resolution.
- **Understanding check and feedback:** a22-check-2; four choices with per-option causal feedback.
- **Recall:** a22-recall-2; question: Without notes, explain: How can a microscope image become reliable quantitative evidence?

#### A2.2.3 — Choose the right microscope 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 60, PDF 72. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** A light microscope can observe many living cells and, with fluorescent stains, can distinguish selected molecules or structures against a background. Immunofluorescence uses antibodies carrying fluorescent labels to localize target antigens; localization depends on antibody specificity, preparation and optical resolution. Conventional light microscopy is limited in spatial resolution by diffraction, so simply enlarging a blurry image does not reveal finer structures.
- **Biological example / application:** To localize a protein, select validated immunofluorescence. To inspect mitochondrial cristae, consider thin-section TEM. Explain how each preparation may introduce artifacts.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-methods
- **Terminology:** TEM; SEM; freeze fracture; cryo-EM; fluorescence; immunofluorescence
- **Misconception to correct:** Higher magnification always produces more genuine detail. → The resolving power of the instrument and specimen preparation set what detail is distinguishable.
- **Further original explanation:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals thin-section internal ultrastructure using transmitted electrons; scanning electron microscopy (SEM) emphasizes specimen surface topography. Electron methods generally need more complex preparation and often cannot examine living cells under conventional high-vacuum conditions. Freeze fracture splits frozen membranes to expose surfaces, while cryogenic electron microscopy rapidly vitrifies material to study structures with reduced crystallization damage. These methods answer different questions, not a universal ranking of 'best microscope'.

#### A2.2.4 — Shared cellular foundations 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 64, PDF 76. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Across living cells, hereditary DNA stores instructions, the cytoplasm provides a water-rich setting for reactions, and a lipid-based plasma membrane separates the cell interior from its environment. Ribosomes synthesize proteins in living cells. These are functional necessities: hereditary information can be copied and interpreted; an aqueous environment allows substances to interact; a boundary makes selective exchange and regulated chemistry possible.
- **Biological example / application:** A tiny cell has DNA, cytoplasm, a lipid membrane and ribosomes but no nucleus. The absence of a nucleus does not make it non-living.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-organization
- **Terminology:** DNA; plasma membrane; cytoplasm; ribosome
- **Misconception to correct:** A nucleus is found in every living cell. → Bacteria and archaea lack nuclei; mature human erythrocytes also lack a nucleus.
- **Further original explanation:** What is shared does not mean every cell has a nucleus, chloroplast, mitochondrion or wall. In eukaryotes the DNA is usually enclosed by a nucleus, whereas prokaryotes lack a membrane-bounded nucleus. Many specialized mature cells may lose typical organelles, as in mammalian red blood cells; such exceptions do not invalidate common evolutionary cellular ancestry. Use the core features to recognize unity while comparing diversity.

#### A2.2.5 — The Gram-positive bacterial model 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 65, PDF 77. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** The syllabus reference model is a Gram-positive eubacterium such as Bacillus or Staphylococcus. A plasma membrane surrounds cytoplasm containing 70S ribosomes; genetic DNA is present in a nucleoid region, commonly as a circular chromosome not enclosed in a nuclear membrane. An external cell wall provides shape and mechanical protection. In Gram-positive bacteria the wall contains a thick peptidoglycan layer; wall and membrane must not be drawn as one structure.
- **Biological example / application:** In a diagram of Bacillus, label an exterior wall, an interior plasma membrane, nucleoid DNA, aqueous cytoplasm and 70S ribosomes. Mark a capsule optional.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-prokaryote
- **Terminology:** Gram-positive; peptidoglycan; nucleoid; 70S ribosomes; plasma membrane
- **Misconception to correct:** All bacteria have flagella, capsules and plasmids. → These features vary; do not add them to every bacterial diagram or require them for identification.
- **Further original explanation:** Some bacteria possess extra surface features such as flagella, capsules or pili, but these are not universal and are not required components of the basic syllabus model. 'Naked DNA' here means DNA is not organized with eukaryotic histone-based chromatin in a nucleus; it does not imply DNA is unassociated with every protein. Prokaryotic cells are not just simplified eukaryotes; their organization and biochemical systems have distinct evolutionary histories.

#### A2.2.6 — Organized eukaryotic compartments 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 67, PDF 79. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Eukaryotic cells have a plasma membrane enclosing a cytoplasm organized by internal membranes. A nucleus contains DNA associated with histone proteins, usually organized as chromosomes; its double nuclear envelope contains pores that regulate traffic. Cytosolic ribosomes are 80S. Mitochondria participate in aerobic respiration; rough endoplasmic reticulum carries ribosomes and supports synthesis of many secreted and membrane proteins; smooth ER participates in lipid synthesis and other functions. The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts and packages cargo; vesicles and lysosomes support transport and degradation.
- **Biological example / application:** A cell that secretes many proteins often has abundant rough ER and Golgi membranes. Predict the relevant structures before labeling a diagram.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-eukaryote
- **Terminology:** nuclear envelope; 80S ribosome; rough ER; Golgi apparatus; microtubule; microfilament
- **Misconception to correct:** A eukaryote is simply any cell with mitochondria. → The nucleus and characteristic compartmentalized organization describe the typical eukaryotic cell; some derived cells have lost mitochondria or nuclei.
- **Further original explanation:** The cytoskeleton includes microtubules and actin microfilaments, which provide organization and help with movement and intracellular transport. Organelle occurrence varies among specialized eukaryotic cell types: chloroplasts are not found in animal cells, and a mature mammalian erythrocyte lacks its nucleus and mitochondria. Do not claim that all eukaryotes are identical or that organelles are floating in an unstructured fluid.

#### A2.2.7 — One cell performs the processes of life 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 74, PDF 86. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** A unicellular organism must perform homeostasis, metabolism, nutrition, movement when relevant, excretion, growth, responses to stimuli and reproduction within one integrated cellular system. Amoeba uses flexible extensions of its surface for movement and engulfment; Paramecium uses cilia and food vacuoles, while many green algae obtain energy by photosynthesis. These are distinct strategies, not a claim that every unicellular organism moves in the same way.
- **Biological example / application:** A Paramecium swims toward a food-rich region, internalizes particles, expels waste and divides. Match each observation to a life process.
- **Figure or microscopy:** Not required for this authored section; drawing and authentic micrograph work remains a separate A2.2.10/11 dependency.
- **Terminology:** homeostasis; metabolism; excretion; stimulus; unicellular
- **Misconception to correct:** Excretion means removing any material the organism did not digest. → Excretion concerns metabolic waste; egestion concerns undigested material.
- **Further original explanation:** Homeostasis involves regulation of internal conditions; metabolism includes chemical reactions supplying materials and energy. Excretion removes metabolic waste rather than undigested food. A ciliated protist can swim toward suitable conditions and away from harmful ones: its response involves sensory and motor cellular processes without a nervous system. Division can produce new organisms because each resulting cell can function independently. The cell remains a coherent organism, not a miniature assemblage of organs.

#### A2.2.8 — Comparing plant, fungal and animal cells 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 75, PDF 87. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Many plant cells have cellulose-based walls, a large permanent sap vacuole and plastids, including chloroplasts in photosynthetic tissues. Fungal cells typically have chitin-rich walls and vacuoles, but do not have chloroplasts. Animal cells lack a rigid cell wall and commonly have small vesicular compartments; many possess centrioles, whereas centrioles are absent from many higher-plant cells. These features fit cellular functions, but individual specialized cells depart from familiar classroom drawings.
- **Biological example / application:** A root cortex cell has a cellulose wall and a large vacuole but no visible chloroplast. It remains a plant cell.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-groups
- **Terminology:** cellulose; chitin; plastid; sap vacuole; centriole; cilia; flagella
- **Misconception to correct:** Every plant cell has chloroplasts and every animal cell has centrioles. → Plastids and centrioles vary with cell lineage and specialization.
- **Further original explanation:** Cell walls are not interchangeable: cellulose, chitin and bacterial peptidoglycan differ in composition. Not every plant cell is photosynthetic: root cells usually lack chloroplasts. Cilia and flagella are present in selected eukaryotic cells or life-cycle stages rather than defining all members of a kingdom. Compare organism type, tissue, life stage and environmental function before declaring a feature universal.

#### A2.2.9 — Cells that break the one-nucleus pattern 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 78, PDF 90. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Typical diagrams show one nucleus per eukaryotic cell, but this is a teaching model rather than a rule. Aseptate fungal hyphae can be continuous cytoplasmic tubes containing many nuclei without complete cross-walls. A skeletal muscle fibre is a very long multinucleate cell produced through fusion of precursor cells. Both cases show how one plasma-membrane-bounded living unit may contain multiple nuclei.
- **Biological example / application:** Classify: aseptate hypha—many nuclei; skeletal muscle fibre—many nuclei; mature human red blood cell—none; mature sieve-tube element—none.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-atypical
- **Terminology:** aseptate hypha; syncytium; erythrocyte; sieve tube element; companion cell
- **Misconception to correct:** Every eukaryotic cell has exactly one nucleus. → Aseptate hyphae and skeletal muscle fibres can be multinucleate; mature erythrocytes and sieve-tube elements lack nuclei.
- **Further original explanation:** A mature mammalian red blood cell has expelled its nucleus, increasing space for haemoglobin and making it flexible, while a mature phloem sieve-tube element loses its nucleus and depends on an associated companion cell for many metabolic functions. A mature sieve element remains alive but is an unusually dependent cell. These are the four syllabus examples: compare them by number of nuclei and organization, rather than incorrectly assuming they represent four different kingdoms.

#### A2.2.10 — Interpret micrograph evidence 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 79, PDF 91. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Interpret a micrograph by reading its scale, microscopy modality, specimen information and visible boundaries before applying any label. In light images, a clear cell wall, large vacuole and chloroplasts may support plant-cell classification; a bacterium may appear as a small featureless rod or sphere because its ribosomes cannot be individually resolved. In thin-section TEM, distinct membranes and electron-density patterns may support identifying the nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast, rough or smooth ER, Golgi stack, vacuole, ribosomes, chromosomes, nucleoid, microvilli or plasma membrane.
- **Biological example / application:** A grayscale TEM shows a double-membrane profile with internal folds. Identify a probable mitochondrion and explain which features support the inference; note that a scale/reference image would strengthen it.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-micrograph-key
- **Terminology:** micrograph; TEM; electron density; cristae; cisterna; nucleoid; microvillus
- **Misconception to correct:** Everything labelled in a cell diagram must be visible in a light micrograph. → Resolution, contrast, preparation and section orientation limit what can be seen; ribosomes normally require electron microscopy.
- **Further original explanation:** Look for clusters of parallel flattened cisternae with attached dark dots when proposing rough ER, stacks of curved smooth cisternae for Golgi, and double membrane plus internal cristae for a mitochondrion. Morphology overlaps and single 2D sections can be ambiguous; an identification is an evidence-based interpretation, not a guess from color. A schematic can train the inference sequence but must never be passed off as an authentic micrograph. The authentic licensed micrograph set is tracked as a required integration asset.

#### A2.2.11 — Scientific drawing from visible structures 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 40, PDF 46; Hodder printed 81, PDF 93. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Start from an authentic source micrograph with a recorded specimen, preparation, technique and scale. Observe the complete object and draw a large clean outline in proportion with unbroken lines. Select only features distinguishable in the source: for example, nuclear envelope, mitochondrion or a region of ER when the micrograph resolves them. Avoid shading, decorative color and microscopic structures that are merely expected from a textbook. State the title, specimen and magnification or a correctly derived scale.
- **Biological example / application:** Self-check an original mitochondrion drawing: outline and folded inner membrane observed; straight leader lines; 'cristae—inner membrane folds associated with ATP production'; no unsupported molecular detail.
- **Figure or microscopy:** Not required for this authored section; drawing and authentic micrograph work remains a separate A2.2.10/11 dependency.
- **Terminology:** observational drawing; leader line; annotation; proportion; scale
- **Misconception to correct:** A good biological drawing should include every organelle known to exist. → Scientific drawing records what is observed; unseen structures must not be invented.
- **Further original explanation:** Annotations should join visible features to their biological functions: a mitochondrion's folded inner membrane supports respiration, while a Golgi stack processes and sorts cellular cargo. A label names a feature; an annotation adds a scientifically supported function. Check each leader line reaches the observed feature, lines do not cross unnecessarily, relative sizes are reasonable, and uncertain structures are identified cautiously. Keyboard-accessible self-review can use a checklist and written rationale; freehand drawing is an optional offline extension.

#### A2.2.12 — Organelles and endosymbiosis (HL)

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 41, PDF 47; Hodder printed 83, PDF 95. Additional higher level.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** The endosymbiotic model proposes that mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from bacteria that were retained within ancestral eukaryotic cells. Its strongest school-level evidence includes small 70S-type ribosomes, their own typically circular DNA and replication by division. Comparative genetic evidence further connects mitochondria to bacterial lineages and chloroplasts to cyanobacteria. Organelles are integrated into their host cells and many essential proteins are now nuclear encoded.
- **Biological example / application:** Evaluate 'Mitochondria contain 70S-like ribosomes, so their bacterial origin is proven by this fact alone.' One observation supports the model, but independent genomic and division evidence strengthen it.
- **Figure or microscopy:** a22-endosymbiosis
- **Terminology:** endosymbiosis; 70S ribosome; circular DNA; phylogenetic evidence; common ancestor
- **Misconception to correct:** Endosymbiosis is a direct photograph of the first eukaryote arising. → It is an evolutionary model supported by multiple independent observations, with unresolved historical details.
- **Further original explanation:** The official sequence describes an early nuclear-bearing, sexually reproducing unicellular common ancestor of eukaryotes, followed by mitochondrial endosymbiosis, with chloroplast endosymbiosis later in particular lineages. This is a historical reconstruction, not a directly recorded event; exact early ordering and the evolution of sex/nucleus remain active research topics. Distinguish evidence (genes, ribosomes, division) from inference (the particular ancestral cell interactions). The theory is compelling because it explains convergent lines of evidence and yields testable predictions.
- **Understanding check and feedback:** a22-check-12; four choices with per-option causal feedback.

#### A2.2.13 — Differentiation builds specialized tissues (HL)

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 41, PDF 47; Hodder printed 86, PDF 98. Additional higher level.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Differentiation is the process by which an initially less-specialized cell acquires a characteristic pattern of structures and activities. In an organism's development, cells inherit broadly the same genome, but regulatory signals alter which genes are expressed. The resulting changes in protein production influence cytoskeleton, membranes, cell shape and physiology; cells with compatible functions can form tissues.
- **Biological example / application:** Two early embryonic cells have the same DNA but receive different morphogen concentrations. Predict how different gene-expression patterns can produce distinct cell types.
- **Figure or microscopy:** Not required for this authored section; drawing and authentic micrograph work remains a separate A2.2.10/11 dependency.
- **Terminology:** differentiation; gene expression; morphogen; tissue
- **Misconception to correct:** Differentiated cells normally specialize by replacing their DNA with different genes. → Different genes are typically switched on or off; changes in gene expression rather than genome replacement direct cell fate.
- **Further original explanation:** Environmental cues include concentration gradients of signalling molecules, interactions with adjacent cells and local extracellular conditions. A precursor cell responding to a positional signal may activate one regulatory program, while a nearby cell follows another; these differences can persist through later divisions. Not every mature specialized cell retains the same DNA complement—for instance, the mature human red blood cell has no nucleus. This distinction prepares for B2.3 without treating differentiation as mutation of the genome.

#### A2.2.14 — Independent origins of multicellularity (HL)

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 41, PDF 47; Hodder printed 87, PDF 99. Additional higher level.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Multicellularity evolved more than once in eukaryotic history. Animals and land plants are multicellular; many fungi and numerous algal groups also show multicellular forms. Multicellular individuals can become larger than typical solitary cells, creating ecological opportunities, and can partition work among cells that specialize in different functions. Coordination and transport become important constraints as body size increases.

### B2.3 — Cell specialization

#### B2.3.1 — Embryonic signals guide cell fate 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 55, PDF 61; Hodder printed 256, PDF 268. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Fertilization produces a zygote, which divides repeatedly to generate initially unspecialized descendant cells. As the embryo develops, cells occupy different positions relative to surrounding tissues and molecular signals. Some signalling molecules (morphogens) form concentration gradients, so cells in different positions experience different concentrations even while inheriting essentially the same genome.
- **Biological example / application:** Two cells sit at different positions in a morphogen gradient. Predict different gene-expression programs and describe how this can create distinct tissue identities.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-gradient

- **Misconception to correct:** Cells differentiate mainly because their DNA is replaced with new genes. → Position-dependent signals alter gene expression while the inherited genome usually remains the same.

Changes in protein abundance and activity then direct differentiation into particular cell types. A high or low morphogen concentration is not itself a different gene; it is contextual information that helps select a cell fate. Because the same genome can support distinct expression programs, a tissue may contain cells with different morphology and roles. Embryogenesis is regulated, not random conversion into arbitrary cell types.

#### B2.3.2 — Stem cells renew and specialize 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 55, PDF 61; Hodder printed 257, PDF 269. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Two capacities define stem cells: self-renewal and differentiation. Self-renewal means cell divisions can yield daughters that retain stem-cell properties; differentiation yields descendants that acquire specialized characteristics. In appropriate biological contexts or culture conditions, stem-cell lineages can undergo extensive or ongoing division. Their capacities are regulated by genetic programs, cellular surroundings and signals, rather than guaranteeing that every stem cell literally proliferates forever.
- **Biological example / application:** A bone marrow stem cell generates another stem cell and a committed blood progenitor. Identify renewal and differentiation in the same division lineage.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-stem
- **Terminology:** stem cell; self-renewal; differentiation; potency; quiescence
- **Misconception to correct:** Any fast-dividing cancer or skin cell must be a stem cell. → Rapid division alone does not establish the ability to self-renew and generate different specialized descendants.

A mature red blood cell cannot divide and is not a stem cell; a bone marrow hematopoietic stem cell can produce both renewing daughters and blood progenitors. Do not confuse potency (range of possible differentiated descendants) with the current speed of division or the number of years a cell survives.

#### B2.3.3 — Stem-cell niches provide regulation 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 55, PDF 61; Hodder printed 258, PDF 270. Shared SL and HL.

#### B2.3.4 — Compare three kinds of potency 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 56, PDF 62; Hodder printed 260, PDF 272. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Potency describes the breadth of cell types a cell can generate under suitable conditions. Totipotent early embryonic cells can contribute to an entire new organism, including the required extraembryonic tissues. The zygote and very early embryonic blastomeres are standard developmental examples, but totipotency is stage- and species-dependent and should not be assigned to an arbitrary cultured cell. Pluripotent cells can generate cell types of all three embryonic germ layers, but cannot alone produce all structures required for a complete developing organism.
- **Biological example / application:** Classify zygote—totipotent; pluripotent embryonic stem cell—pluripotent; marrow blood-forming stem cell—multipotent. Explain each by descendants it can form.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-potency
- **Terminology:** totipotent; pluripotent; multipotent; blastomere; inner cell mass
- **Misconception to correct:** Pluripotent and totipotent both mean a cell can make an entire organism on its own. → Totipotency includes the ability to generate all necessary embryonic and extraembryonic lineages; pluripotency does not.
- **Further original explanation:** Multipotent adult tissue stem cells generate a more limited family of descendants. A hematopoietic stem cell in bone marrow can ultimately produce several types of blood cell, but ordinarily not neural tissue. In an early animal embryo, developmental potential becomes restricted as embryonic lineages separate. The inner cell mass of a mammalian blastocyst contains pluripotent progenitors, not a cluster of uniformly totipotent cells. Potency and differentiation state are related but distinct measurements.

#### B2.3.5 — Different sizes for different jobs 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 56, PDF 62; Hodder printed 261, PDF 273. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Human cells vary greatly in their dimensions and shape because they perform different functions. A mature human erythrocyte is a small flexible biconcave disc that can pass through narrow capillaries, whereas a mature human egg cell has a large volume and cytoplasmic resources important for early development. Sperm are smaller cells with a compact head and flagellum for movement through the female reproductive tract. White blood cells vary in size, shape and behavior according to type.
- **Biological example / application:** Explain why a 100 µm-diameter human egg and a 7–8 µm-wide red blood cell serve different functions; dimensions are approximate and not fixed biological constants.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-sizes

Their linear length should not be mistaken for a comparably huge spherical volume. Size changes diffusion distances and supply demands, but cell function also depends on membrane folding, internal transport, metabolic activity and tissue context. #### B2.3.6 — Why exchange area limits cell size 

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 56, PDF 62; Hodder printed 263, PDF 275. Shared SL and HL.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** For a cube with side length L, surface area = 6L² and volume = L³, so the ratio SA:V = 6/L. A cube of side 1 µm has 6 µm² surface area and 1 µm³ volume, giving 6:1 µm⁻¹. At side 2 µm, area is 24 µm² and volume 8 µm³, ratio 3:1 µm⁻¹. At side 4 µm, area is 96 µm² and volume 64 µm³, ratio 1.5:1 µm⁻¹. Doubling size multiplies area by four but volume by eight.
- **Biological example / application:** For a cube of side 3 µm: area = 6×3² = 54 µm²; volume = 3³ = 27 µm³; SA:V = 2 µm⁻¹. Doubling to side 6 gives area 216 µm², volume 216 µm³, SA:V = 1 µm⁻¹.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-cubes
- **Terminology:** surface area; volume; SA:V ratio; square law; cube law; model assumption
- **Misconception to correct:** Doubling the side length doubles both area and volume. → Area scales with side length squared and volume with its cube; SA:V falls as 1/L.
- **Further original explanation:** Substances cross the cell membrane across surface area, while total requirements for nutrients, gas exchange and waste clearance often increase with metabolically active volume. Therefore, if membrane exchange capacity per unit area and demand per volume stayed equal, a larger cell would have less exchange capacity relative to demand. Real cells are not cubes: folds, shape, transport proteins, metabolic rates and cytoplasmic organization alter performance. The cube model isolates the scaling rule without claiming that every biological limitation has one simple cause.
- **Understanding check and feedback:** b23-check-6; four choices with per-option causal feedback.
- **Recall:** b23-recall-6; question: Retrieve without notes: Why does increasing linear size reduce relative exchange area?

#### B2.3.7 — Increase exchange surface by shaping cells (HL)

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 56, PDF 62; Hodder printed 266, PDF 278. Additional higher level.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Flattening a cell increases surface area relative to its volume for a given amount of internal material and can shorten diffusion distance. A mature human red blood cell's biconcave shape makes a relatively high membrane area accessible to gas exchange, while its thin central region reduces diffusion path length. A flattened shape is not the only possible adaptation, and erythrocytes also rely on hemoglobin and flexibility.
- **Biological example / application:** A proximal tubule epithelial cell increases apical microvilli. Predict greater membrane area available for reabsorption, assuming transport-protein density remains comparable.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-surface
- **Terminology:** biconcave; microvilli; invagination; proximal convoluted tubule; erythrocyte
- **Misconception to correct:** A microvillus is a motile flagellum that pumps molecules. → Microvilli are short surface projections that enlarge membrane area; cilia and flagella have different architectures and functions.

Invaginations are inward folds of the membrane that create additional membrane area without equivalent increases in total cellular volume. Both adaptations are meaningful when the appropriate exchange/transport proteins are present; more membrane alone does not guarantee unlimited transport. Distinguish microvilli from motile cilia and describe the physical geometry involved.

#### B2.3.8 — Two cell designs in an alveolus (HL)

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 56, PDF 62; Hodder printed 267, PDF 279. Additional higher level.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** The alveolar epithelium contains cells specialized for complementary tasks. Type I pneumocytes spread into extremely thin, flat sheets lining most of the air-exchange surface; their narrow cytoplasmic regions help minimize diffusion distance between alveolar air and the capillary blood barrier. Type II pneumocytes are more cuboidal and contain many lamellar bodies—secretory organelles holding components of pulmonary surfactant.
- **Biological example / application:** In a patient with reduced surfactant production, predict a higher tendency for alveoli to collapse despite continued thinness of type I cell sheets.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-alveoli
- **Terminology:** alveolus; type I pneumocyte; type II pneumocyte; lamellar body; surfactant
- **Misconception to correct:** Type I pneumocytes produce most surfactant and type II pneumocytes form the thinnest diffusion barrier. → Type I cells are thin for gas diffusion; type II cells store and secrete surfactant from lamellar bodies.
- **Further original explanation:** Type II pneumocytes release surfactant into the alveolar lumen. Surfactant reduces surface tension at the air–liquid interface and helps keep alveoli from collapsing during breathing. The thin barrier produced by type I cells and surfactant secretion by type II cells are related to the overall function of the tissue, but must not be confused: type I is not the principal surfactant secretor. Lamellar bodies represent intracellular secretory structure; they are not empty alveolar air spaces. Diagram thickness is exaggerated for visibility and not a literal microscopic scale.

#### B2.3.9 — Cardiac cells versus skeletal muscle fibres (HL)

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 56, PDF 62; Hodder printed 268, PDF 280. Additional higher level.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** Both cardiac muscle cells and skeletal striated muscle fibres contain contractile myofibrils built of repeated sarcomeres. Cardiac muscle cells usually branch, connect end-to-end through intercalated discs and typically have one central nucleus, although some are binucleate. Their network assists coordinated rhythmic contraction. Skeletal muscle fibres are long, generally unbranched structures formed by fusion of myoblasts; they contain many nuclei positioned near their periphery.
- **Biological example / application:** A micrograph shows branching striated fibres with central nuclei and visible junctions. Reason that it likely depicts cardiac tissue, giving at least two visible clues.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-muscle
- **Terminology:** cardiomyocyte; striated muscle fibre; myofibril; sarcomere; intercalated disc; syncytium
- **Misconception to correct:** A skeletal muscle fibre is a collection of many separate cells merely lying next to one another. → Its multiple nuclei occupy a continuous fused cell bounded by one sarcolemma.
- **Further original explanation:** A skeletal striated muscle fibre is a single highly specialized multinucleated cell, not a string of numerous ordinary single-nucleus cells. The relative length, branching and nuclei number are part of structural explanations for tissue function, but students should distinguish observations from proposed functional hypotheses. Both tissues appear striated because of the organization of myofibrils. Neither should be depicted as merely an elongated generic animal cell with a single oval nucleus.

#### B2.3.10 — Human gametes: structure and reproduction (HL)

- **Authority and level:** Guide printed 56, PDF 62; Hodder printed 271, PDF 283. Additional higher level.
- **Required mechanism and concepts:** A human sperm cell is comparatively small and motile. It has a streamlined head containing a haploid nucleus and an acrosome with proteins involved in interacting with the egg's surrounding layers. Mitochondria concentrated in the midpiece support energy demands of flagellar movement, while the flagellum generates propulsion. These features help sperm reach and interact with the egg and deliver paternal genetic material.
- **Biological example / application:** A sperm with an immotile flagellum may have reduced capacity to reach the oocyte; an oocyte contains much more cytoplasm but not double the sperm's haploid nuclear chromosome set.
- **Figure or microscopy:** b23-gametes

- **Misconception to correct:** The egg is larger because it contains the full diploid genome while sperm carries only half. → Human gametes are haploid; the egg's larger cytoplasm supports early developmental processes.
- **Further original explanation:** A human secondary oocyte is large and nonmotile, with a haploid chromosome complement arrested at metaphase II until fertilization triggers completion of meiosis. Its abundant cytoplasm supplies molecules and machinery to the early embryo; its plasma membrane and surrounding zona pellucida participate in fertilization interactions. Egg size should not be attributed to containing much more DNA than the sperm: both gametes carry haploid nuclear genomes. Simplified drawings should not show an adult ovulated human egg as already having completed meiosis II before fertilization.
